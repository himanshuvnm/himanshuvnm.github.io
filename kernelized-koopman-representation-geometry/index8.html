Symmetry and Geometry in Neural Representations

Kernelized Koopman Representation Geometry Governs Transient Amplification in Neural Dynamical Systems

The same neural dynamics can yield dramatically different finite-time behavior when represented in different reproducing kernel Hilbert spaces.

Research motivation · Why this paper?

Two models can reproduce the same neural trajectories yet respond very differently in representation space.

Data-driven Koopman models are often evaluated through prediction error, reconstructed spectra, and eigenfunctions. But these diagnostics do not, by themselves, characterize how a learned operator behaves over finite time.

THE BLIND SPOT

Stable-looking spectra are not enough

Non-normal operators may exhibit large transient growth even when their eigenvalues suggest asymptotic stability. Such amplification can affect correlations and perturbation-response calculations.

THE HIDDEN CHOICE

A kernel chooses a geometry

Kernel Extended Dynamic Mode Decomposition (KEDMD) represents observables in a reproducing kernel Hilbert space. Different kernels induce different Gram matrices, norms, and finite-dimensional surrogate operators.

THE RESEARCH QUESTION

Is a Koopman surrogate representation-neutral?

Given the same neural dynamics and snapshot pairs, how does kernel choice alter finite-time operator growth, correlation amplification, and resolvent sensitivity?

\[\text{Same neural dynamics and data}\;\not\Rightarrow\;\text{same finite-time representation response}.\]
What the paper investigates

Beyond eigenvalues

We examine regularized KEDMD surrogates using Gram-induced operator norms, correlation-based transient amplification, pseudospectra, and grid-based Kreiss constant estimates. The study compares four kernels across linear excitatory–inhibitory, Wilson–Cowan, and FitzHugh–Nagumo dynamics.

Why it matters

Representation quality is dynamical

Two kernel surrogates should not be treated as interchangeable merely because they use the same trajectories or have similar spectra. Before using a surrogate for prediction or perturbation-response analysis, we should inspect its finite-time amplification and resolvent behavior.

Scope: these findings concern finite-dimensional, regularized kernelized Koopman representations; they do not imply that the physical neural dynamics themselves change when a kernel is changed. This motivation follows the accepted paper's Introduction and problem statement.

01 / Central discovery

Eigenvalues alone do not tell the whole story.

Kernel choice changes the geometry in which a data-driven Koopman surrogate is measured. Even with nearly unit spectral radii, the resulting finite-time operator growth can differ substantially.

02 / Reproducing kernel geometry

Four kernels. Four ways of measuring similarity.

The kernel is not merely a computational ingredient: it specifies the inner-product geometry of the sampled representation. The four families below are exactly those used in the accompanying kernel-section visualization code.

How to read the original scientific figure. Color encodes similarity to the anchor \(z_0=(0.88,0.14)\); white curves are the \(0.2,0.4,0.6,0.8\) similarity contours; white arrows point along \(+\nabla_x k(x,z_0)\), toward increasing similarity. The code uses a common maximum gradient magnitude for cross-kernel arrow scaling, followed by square-root compression and a minimum display length. These arrows are kernel gradients, not trajectories of the neural dynamical system.

01 · Coordinate-sensitive locality

Laplace · \(L^1\) distance

\[k_{\mathrm L}(x,z)=\exp\!\left(-\frac{\|x-z\|_1}{\sigma}\right)\]

Diamond-shaped similarity contours arise from the sum of absolute coordinate differences. The section is not differentiable along coordinate lines passing through the anchor.

Interpretation: coordinatewise sensitivity and sharp local similarity. This is the specific \(L^1\)-Laplace variant used by the supplied plotting code.

02 · Moderately smooth locality

Matérn-3/2

\[k_{\mathrm M}(x,z)=\left(1+\frac{\sqrt3\,r}{\sigma}\right)e^{-\sqrt3\,r/\sigma},\quad r=\|x-z\|_2\]

Circular contours and a smooth first derivative reflect radial similarity with limited smoothness relative to the Gaussian.

Interpretation: a balance of locality and regularity; not a guarantee of better numerical conditioning.

03 · Multiscale similarity

Rational Quadratic

\[k_{\mathrm{RQ}}(x,z)=\left(1+\frac{\|x-z\|_2^2}{2\alpha\sigma^2}\right)^{-\alpha}\]

Algebraic tails retain similarity farther from the anchor than exponentially decaying sections. The shape parameter \(\alpha\) changes the relative contribution of spatial scales.

Interpretation: multiscale radial coupling. The original figure uses \(\alpha=1\).

04 · Infinitely smooth similarity

Gaussian RBF

\[k_{\mathrm G}(x,z)=\exp\!\left(-\frac{\|x-z\|_2^2}{2\sigma^2}\right)\]

Its circular contours reflect Euclidean radial similarity and rapid decay away from the anchor. The corresponding RKHS consists of highly regular functions.

Interpretation: smoothness is a function-space property, not a promise of low transient amplification.

From similarity to dynamics

Why this matters for Koopman representations

Given sampled states \(x_1,\ldots,x_m\), each kernel determines a Gram matrix \(G_k=[k(x_i,x_j)]\). With regularization, the corresponding normalized fitted operator is

\[\widetilde A_{k,\lambda}=G_{k,\lambda}^{1/2}A_{k,\lambda}G_{k,\lambda}^{-1/2},\qquad \mathcal G_k(T)=\max_{0\le t\le T}\|\widetilde A_{k,\lambda}^{\,t}\|_2.\]

The same physical dynamics can have different fitted kernel representations and different measured finite-time growth. Contour shape alone does not establish an amplification ranking; that evidence comes from the KEDMD experiments and Kreiss analysis.

04 / Mathematical foundations

Why does kernel geometry govern transient amplification?

The theory answers a sequence of questions: how the same physical map transports kernel sections, how the resulting geometry enters a finite-dimensional Koopman approximation, and why that representation can exhibit large short-time growth even when its eigenvalues look benign.

The motivating difficulty

Prediction and eigenvalues do not tell the whole story.

Consider the same neural system, the same observed snapshot pairs, and several different kernels. A kernel changes the RKHS in which the data are represented, the associated Gram matrix, and the fitted KEDMD surrogate. Consequently, two surrogates may have similar spectral locations but very different norms of their powers. The central mathematical task is to trace this discrepancy from kernel geometry to operator growth and finally to observable correlations.

The analysis concerns finite-dimensional learned representations. A large surrogate norm does not, by itself, imply a comparably large physical-state perturbation or an instability of the original neural dynamics.

Starting point · One physical system, several representations

From state evolution to kernel coordinates

The physical system evolves as \(x_{t+1}=F(x_t)\). Its Koopman operator acts on observables, \((Uf)(x)=f(F(x))\). The RKHS reproducing identity \(\langle f,k_x\rangle_{\mathcal H_k}=f(x)\) leads, under the paper's domain assumptions, to the kernel-section transport relation

\[U^*k_x=k_{F(x)}.\]

Why this matters: it converts the evolution of an observable into a statement about how kernel sections move. The before-and-after Gram matrices then measure this deformation without requiring a global coordinate system for the full RKHS.

Physical map \(F\)→Section transport \(T_{F,X}\)→Regularized factor \(\chi_{k,\lambda}\)→Normalized surrogate \(\widetilde A_\lambda\)→Growth and correlations
Question 1 · Why transport?

Compare geometry before and after the same dynamics.

For the snapshot sets \(X=\{x_i\}\) and \(Y=\{F(x_i)\}\), the matrices \(G_X\) and \(G_Y\) encode pairwise kernel-section inner products. The map \(T_{F,X}\) carries each section \(k_{x_i}\) to \(k_{F(x_i)}\). Its squared norm is the largest possible ratio of transported to initial RKHS energy:

\[\frac{\|\sum_i c_i k_{F(x_i)}\|_{\mathcal H_k}^2}{\|\sum_i c_i k_{x_i}\|_{\mathcal H_k}^2}=\frac{c^*G_Yc}{c^*G_Xc}.\]

The maximization of this quotient motivates Proposition 5. The result is a generalized eigenvalue problem, not a claim that the physical dynamics changed when the kernel changed.

Question 2 · Why normalize?

Euclidean coefficient length is not RKHS function length.

The coefficient vector \(v\) represents a kernel expansion, whose geometry is measured by \(\|v\|_{G_\lambda}^2=v^*G_\lambda v\), rather than by \(v^*v\). Regularization makes \(G_\lambda=G_X+\lambda I\) positive definite. The coordinate change \(u=G_\lambda^{1/2}v\) converts the induced norm into an ordinary matrix 2-norm:

\[\|A_\lambda^t\|_{G_\lambda}=\|\widetilde A_\lambda^t\|_2,\qquad \widetilde A_\lambda=G_\lambda^{1/2}A_\lambda G_\lambda^{-1/2}.\]

Why this matters: the normalized operator is the object whose transient growth, pseudospectra, and Kreiss constant can be compared consistently in the induced geometry. This is the central change of coordinates in Theorem 10.

Question 3 · Why study correlations?

Connect operator growth to an observable quantity.

The paper begins with time correlations of observables, then introduces their finite-dimensional KEDMD counterpart \(C_f^{(\lambda)}(t)=v^*G_\lambda A_\lambda^t v\). Cauchy–Schwarz bounds the correlation magnitude by the initial coefficient energy multiplied by the induced operator growth.

\[\frac{|C_f^{(\lambda)}(t)|}{C_f^{(\lambda)}(0)}\leq\|A_\lambda^t\|_{G_\lambda},\qquad v\neq0.\]

Interpretation: the operator norm bounds the worst-case response, but a particular observable need not attain it. Corollary 11 sharpens the connection by identifying the supremum of normalized self-correlations with the numerical radius.

Question 4 · Why non-normality and the Kreiss constant?

Spectral stability is not a finite-time growth guarantee.

For a normal normalized matrix, the norm of its powers is determined by its spectral radius. A non-normal matrix can have eigenvalues inside the unit disk while its powers first grow and only later decay. This motivates two complementary diagnostics: direct finite-time power norms and resolvent-based pseudospectral analysis.

\[\rho(\widetilde A_\lambda)\quad\text{versus}\quad\max_{0\le t\le T}\|\widetilde A_\lambda^t\|_2\quad\text{and}\quad\mathcal K(\widetilde A_\lambda).\]

Theorem 10 connects these quantities under stated hypotheses. The Kreiss laboratory then tests the distinction using numerical estimates from the three neural-system experiments.

Formal results · Accepted manuscript

The precise mathematical statements.

The cards below collect the results in the order used in the paper. The explanations above provide the motivation; these formulas state what is actually proved or bounded.

Proposition 5 · Finite kernel-section transport

Geometry deformation under the same physical map

On the sampled span \(V_X=\mathrm{span}\{k_{x_i}\}\), the section-transport map sends \(\sum_i c_i k_{x_i}\) to \(\sum_i c_i k_{F(x_i)}\). For invertible \(G_X\),

\[\|T_{F,X}\|_{\mathcal H_k\to\mathcal H_k}^2=\lambda_{\max}\!\left(G_X^{-1/2}G_YG_X^{-1/2}\right).\]

Proof idea. The squared ratio of image and source RKHS norms is \(c^*G_Yc/(c^*G_Xc)\); maximize the generalized Rayleigh quotient. If \(G_X\) is singular, well-definedness and pseudoinverse treatment require additional conditions.

Definition 8 · Regularized transport

The geometry-sensitive transport factor

\[\chi_{k,\lambda}(F;X)=\left\|G_\lambda^{-1/2}G_YG_\lambda^{-1/2}\right\|_2^{1/2}.\]

This factor measures regularized transport deformation. It enters the normalized KEDMD growth bound below.

Proposition 9 · Correlation response

Observable-level amplification

For \(C_f^{(\lambda)}(t)=v^*G_\lambda A_\lambda^t v\), the induced norm gives

\[\left|C_f^{(\lambda)}(t)\right|\leq\|v\|_{G_\lambda}^2\|A_\lambda^t\|_{G_\lambda}.\]

This follows from Cauchy–Schwarz in the \(G_\lambda\) inner product and the definition of the induced operator norm.

Theorem 10 · Normalized growth and spectral diagnostics

Why the kernel-induced norm matters

\[\|\widetilde A_\lambda\|_2\leq\chi_{k,\lambda},\qquad \|A_\lambda^t\|_{G_\lambda}=\|\widetilde A_\lambda^t\|_2\leq\chi_{k,\lambda}^{\,t}.\]

When \(\widetilde A_\lambda\) is normal, \(\|\widetilde A_\lambda^t\|_2=\rho(\widetilde A_\lambda)^t\). For non-normal surrogates, eigenvalues alone do not control finite-time growth.

The Kreiss and pseudospectral implications of Theorem 10 are developed in the dedicated laboratory below. The finite-dimensional Kreiss upper bound requires the spectrum to lie in the closed unit disk.

Corollary 11 · Numerical radius

Normalized correlation response

\[\sup_{v\ne0}\frac{|v^*G_\lambda A_\lambda^t v|}{v^*G_\lambda v}=w(\widetilde A_\lambda^t)\leq\|\widetilde A_\lambda^t\|_2\leq\chi_{k,\lambda}^{\,t}.\]

The equality follows by the change of variables \(u=G_\lambda^{1/2}v\); the inequality uses the numerical radius bound.

05 / Kreiss constant and pseudospectral analysis

The resolvent reveals what the spectrum conceals.

Unlike a spectral-radius diagnostic, the Kreiss quantity responds to non-normal resolvent amplification. Here we use the paper's grid-based estimates, and retain the exact distinction between the sampled numerical maximum and the continuous Kreiss constant.

\[\mathcal K(B)=\sup_{|z|>1}(|z|-1)\|(zI-B)^{-1}\|_2,\qquad \widehat{\mathcal K}_{\mathcal G}(B)=\max_{\substack{z\in\mathcal G\\|z|>1}}\frac{|z|-1}{\sigma_{\min}(zI-B)}.\]
THEOREM 10(5) · KREISS MATRIX THEOREM

Resolvent and power growth

\[\mathcal K(B)\leq\sup_{t\geq0}\|B^t\|_2\leq e\,n\,\mathcal K(B)\]

For an \(n\times n\) matrix with spectrum in the closed unit disk. Some fitted surrogates in the paper have spectral radii slightly above one, so the right-hand finite upper bound must not be applied to those matrices without modification.

PSEUDOSPECTRAL EXTENT

Outward pseudospectral inflation

\[\mathcal K(B)\geq\varepsilon^{-1}(r_\varepsilon(B)-1)\]

Here \(r_\varepsilon\) is the radial extent of the \(\varepsilon\)-pseudospectrum. This lower bound is informative when the pseudospectrum extends beyond the unit circle.

Appendix D · Numerical methodology

How the estimates were computed

Construct \(\widetilde A_\lambda\), sample \(z\) on a Cartesian grid over \([-1.3,1.3]^2\), retain \(|z|>1\), evaluate \(\sigma_{\min}(zI-\widetilde A_\lambda)\), then maximize \((|z|-1)/\sigma_{\min}\).

The study checks 31×31, 61×61 and 121×121 grids. The paper reports that 61→121 refinement changes estimates by at most approximately 1.1%, with the kernel ordering preserved.

Interpretation · Not an equality

Kreiss is not the transient peak

The discrete Kreiss estimate is a resolvent diagnostic, not a direct prediction of the peak power norm. The kernel ordering agrees across these experiments, but numerical values differ and the true supremum is not generally attained on the finite grid.

A finite-horizon peak is also not necessarily the infinite-horizon supremum appearing in the Kreiss matrix theorem.

06 / Interactive mathematical illustration

How can a spectrally stable system amplify a perturbation?

This is a controlled two-dimensional matrix example, not a measurement from the neural-system experiments. It isolates non-normal coupling, the mechanism behind transient growth, while keeping every eigenvalue fixed.

The question being tested

Does a stable spectrum guarantee that every perturbation immediately decays?

No. Eigenvalues determine asymptotic stability, but the singular values of matrix powers determine the largest finite-time amplification of an initial perturbation. Those are different mathematical questions.

\[u_{t+1}=B_\alpha u_t,\qquad B_\alpha=\begin{pmatrix}0.8&\alpha\\0&0.8\end{pmatrix},\qquad \rho(B_\alpha)=0.8<1.\]

Both eigenvalues are always 0.8. The parameter \(\alpha\) changes only the off-diagonal coupling. When \(\alpha=0\), the operator is normal and every state contracts by \(0.8\) per step. When \(\alpha\neq0\), the matrix is non-normal, and its coordinate coupling can temporarily transfer energy into an amplified direction.

01 · What the curve actually measures

The worst-case perturbation at each time

\[\|B_\alpha^t\|_2=\max_{\|u_0\|_2=1}\|B_\alpha^tu_0\|_2=\sigma_{\max}(B_\alpha^t).\]

The plotted curve is not one arbitrary trajectory. At each time \(t\), it measures the maximum amplification over all unit initial perturbations. The maximizing direction can depend on time.

02 · Why a transient peak appears

A polynomial factor competes with exponential decay

\[B_\alpha^t=\begin{pmatrix}0.8^t&t\alpha\,0.8^{t-1}\\0&0.8^t\end{pmatrix},\quad t\ge1.\]

The off-diagonal term initially grows in proportion to \(t\), while the factor \(0.8^{t-1}\) eventually decays. Their competition creates a finite-time peak even though every eigenvalue lies strictly inside the unit circle.

03 · What changing α teaches

Eigenvalues alone are incomplete

Move the slider from \(\alpha=0\) to a positive value. The spectral radius remains \(0.8\), but the largest singular value of a matrix power can become greater than one. A system can therefore be asymptotically stable yet strongly amplify some perturbations before they decay.

04 · What the illustration does not prove

It is a mechanism, not a kernel comparison

The matrix \(B_\alpha\) is a synthetic example in Euclidean geometry. It does not compute a kernel Gram matrix, fit KEDMD, or reproduce any numerical amplification values from the paper. Its purpose is to make the spectral-versus-transient distinction transparent.

Connection to the paper

From a 2×2 example to kernelized Koopman representations

In the paper, the finite-dimensional fitted operator is assessed in a kernel-induced geometry, rather than by treating arbitrary coefficient coordinates as intrinsically Euclidean. The relevant normalized matrix takes the form

\[\widetilde A_{k,\lambda}=G_{k,\lambda}^{1/2}A_{k,\lambda}G_{k,\lambda}^{-1/2},\qquad \max_{0\le t\le T}\|\widetilde A_{k,\lambda}^{\,t}\|_2.\]

The illustration explains why we measure powers of the normalized operator and examine non-normality: a stable spectrum by itself does not determine the transient response. The experiments, rather than this toy model, establish how that response varies across kernels and neural systems.

Takeaway: Spectral stability concerns the long run. Transient amplification concerns the largest possible short-run response. Kernel geometry affects the latter through the fitted representation and its norm.
