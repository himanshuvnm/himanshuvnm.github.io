A geometric perspective on generative weather ensembles: rather than matching only scalar spread, study how forecast uncertainty responds to observation-space directions.

01 / The motivation

Why matching spread is not enough.

Weather ensemble forecasts are useful not merely because they quantify uncertainty, but because they describe how uncertainty is organized across variables, locations, and scales. In data assimilation, those relationships determine how a sparse set of observations changes an entire atmospheric state.

01 · Marginal uncertainty

How large is the spread?

Variance and ensemble spread describe the magnitude of forecast uncertainty. They are essential diagnostics, but do not determine correlations or covariance orientation.

02 · Joint geometry

How do errors move together?

Covariance encodes cross-variable and cross-location relationships. Different covariance matrices can share the same diagonal, and therefore the same marginal spread.

03 · Observation impact

Where does information travel?

The Kalman gain uses cross-covariance to propagate information from observation space back into the full state. Geometric mismatch can change that propagation.

\[B_1=\begin{bmatrix}1&0\\0&1\end{bmatrix},\qquad B_2=\begin{bmatrix}1&0.8\\0.8&1\end{bmatrix}\]Both covariances are positive definite and have identical diagonal variances and trace, but different correlations.

One observation, two different updates

Suppose we observe only the first coordinate, with \(H=[1\;0]\) and observation-error variance \(R=1\). Then

\[K_1=\begin{bmatrix}0.5\\0\end{bmatrix},\qquad K_2=\begin{bmatrix}0.5\\0.4\end{bmatrix}\]

The second covariance updates the unobserved coordinate because its errors are correlated with the observed one. Identical spread does not imply identical analysis behavior.

The challenge for generative ensembles

A conditional generative model may produce ensembles with realistic average spread while missing directional covariance structure. A spread-only criterion cannot distinguish the two matrices above.

That motivates a more discriminating question: does the generated covariance act like the reference covariance on the directions relevant to assimilation?

The inverse question driving this project. Instead of asking only whether a generated ensemble has the right uncertainty amount, ask whether it produces the right uncertainty response when probed by observation-space or random directions.

02 / Mathematical foundations

From ensemble anomalies to assimilation-relevant covariance.

The key objects are an ensemble-based background covariance, its action on test vectors, and the cross-covariance terms appearing in the Kalman gain. Their matrix-free forms make high-dimensional evaluation possible.

Step 1 · Ensemble statistics

Constructing the sample covariance

For \(m\ge 2\) ensemble members \(x^{(j)}\in\mathbb R^n\), define the ensemble mean and anomaly matrix

\[\bar x=\frac1m\sum_{j=1}^m x^{(j)},\qquad X=\big[x^{(1)}-\bar x,\ldots,x^{(m)}-\bar x\big].\]
\[B=\frac{XX^{\mathsf T}}{m-1}\]

\(B\) is symmetric and positive semidefinite. Since centered anomalies sum to zero, \(\operatorname{rank}(B)\le m-1\), typically far below the state dimension \(n\).

Step 2 · Matrix-free evaluation

Apply covariance without forming it

For any state-space direction \(v\in\mathbb R^n\), associativity gives

\[Bv=\frac{X(X^{\mathsf T}v)}{m-1}.\]

First compute \(X^{\mathsf T}v\in\mathbb R^m\), then multiply by \(X\). This requires anomaly-matrix products, not storage of an \(n\times n\) covariance matrix.

For \(q\) probes, the same operation becomes \(BV=X(X^{\mathsf T}V)/(m-1)\).

Step 3 · Observation geometry

Projecting uncertainty into observation space

Let \(H\in\mathbb R^{p\times n}\) be a linear observation operator and \(R\in\mathbb R^{p\times p}\) a positive-definite observation-error covariance.

\[BH^{\mathsf T}=\frac{X(HX)^{\mathsf T}}{m-1}\]
\[HBH^{\mathsf T}=\frac{(HX)(HX)^{\mathsf T}}{m-1}\]

These are the state–observation cross-covariance and the predicted observation covariance, respectively.

Step 4 · Assimilation update

Why the Kalman gain matters

\[K=BH^{\mathsf T}(HBH^{\mathsf T}+R)^{-1}\]
\[x^{a}=x^{b}+K(y-Hx^{b})\]

Here \(x^b\) is the background estimate, \(y\) the observation vector, and \(x^a\) the analysis estimate. Different covariance responses can produce different state updates even when overall ensemble spread matches.

Step 5 · What randomized probes can identify

A matrix-free discrepancy identity

Let \(\Delta B=B_{\mathrm{gen}}-B_{\mathrm{ref}}\). If a probe \(v\) has second moment \(\mathbb E[vv^{\mathsf T}]=I\), then

\[\mathbb E_v\|\Delta Bv\|_2^2=\operatorname{tr}(\Delta B^{\mathsf T}\Delta B)=\|\Delta B\|_F^2.\]

This identity explains how covariance-response comparisons can detect matrix-level differences through vector products. It is an exact expectation identity; with finitely many random probes, the corresponding sample average is an estimator with sampling variability.

For observation-focused probes of the form \(v=H^{\mathsf T}u\), the response becomes \(\Delta B H^{\mathsf T}u\), directly testing the cross-covariance action relevant to the gain. Such probes do not necessarily identify covariance differences invisible to \(H\).

Important distinction. Covariance-response agreement, spread calibration, and analysis-RMSE improvement are different criteria. The mathematical identities above motivate evaluation; they do not, by themselves, guarantee improved assimilation skill or reproduce a specific training objective from the accepted paper.

All formulas in this section use a linear observation operator and sample-ensemble covariance. For nonlinear observation maps, analogous constructions generally use a linearization or ensemble observation anomalies.

04 / Interactive mathematical laboratory

Same spread. Different assimilation response.

This self-contained two-dimensional example is an illustration, not an experimental result from the accepted paper. For a fixed anisotropy ratio, rotation preserves the eigenvalues and total variance. Changing the anisotropy slider changes the eigenvalues and total variance.

Exactly what the sliders construct

The first slider rotates the principal axes of a symmetric positive-definite covariance; the second changes the leading eigenvalue. The observation vector is normalized, and observation-error variance is set to one.

\[B(\theta,a)=R_\theta\begin{bmatrix}a&0\\0&1\end{bmatrix}R_\theta^{\mathsf T},\qquad R_\theta=\begin{bmatrix}\cos\theta&-\sin\theta\\\sin\theta&\cos\theta\end{bmatrix}\]
\[h\in\left\{\begin{bmatrix}1\\0\end{bmatrix},\begin{bmatrix}0\\1\end{bmatrix},\frac{1}{\sqrt 2}\begin{bmatrix}1\\1\end{bmatrix}\right\},\qquad q=h^{\mathsf T}Bh\]
\[\underbrace{Bh}_{\text{covariance response}},\qquad\underbrace{K=\frac{Bh}{h^{\mathsf T}Bh+1}}_{\text{Kalman gain for one scalar observation}}\]

Here the observation operator is the row vector \(H=h^{\mathsf T}\). The covariance ellipse visualizes the level set \(x^{\mathsf T}B^{-1}x=1\). The drawn arrows are direction-normalized for readability; their numerical magnitudes are shown in the metric cards.

06 / Scientific evaluation

From ensemble calibration to assimilation skill.

A rigorous evaluation distinguishes uncertainty magnitude, covariance geometry, and downstream analysis quality. These are related but not interchangeable.

Ensemble statistics

Spread and reliability

Ensemble spread, ensemble-mean RMSE, and spread-to-error ratios describe calibration but do not fully identify covariance.

Covariance structure

Response and projection

Compare covariance-vector responses, \(BH^{\mathsf T}\), \(HBH^{\mathsf T}\), and their sensitivity to observation geometry.

Downstream utility

Analysis behavior

Measure Kalman-gain discrepancy and analysis RMSE in controlled data-assimilation experiments.
