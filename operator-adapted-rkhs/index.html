Operator-Adapted Reproducing Kernel Geometries for Structure-Preserving Representation Learning

What if the operator is fixed, but the geometry is ours to design? An exact existence and classification theory for Hilbert-space geometries in which a prescribed linear operator becomes norm preserving.

Choose a geometry first, then ask whether an operator respects it.

\(G\ \longrightarrow\ M\)

01 / The motivation

Preserve the operator by redesigning the geometry.

Most representation-learning methods modify features or fit a new transformation. This work reverses the question: given an operator, can we design the inner product—and therefore an RKHS—in which that operator acts as an exact isometry?

The obstacle

Euclidean distortion

A matrix may have all eigenvalues on the unit circle and still stretch some vectors in the usual Euclidean norm. Eigenvalue magnitudes do not by themselves determine whether lengths and angles are preserved.

The design variable

A positive-definite metric

Instead of altering the dynamics, choose a Hermitian matrix \(G\succ0\) that defines the inner product. Exact invariance is expressed by \(M^*GM=G\).

The payoff

A family of compatible kernels

Each admissible metric induces a reproducing kernel on the chosen feature space. The question is not merely whether a solution exists, but how to characterize all compatible geometries.

The conceptual starting point

Why can the same operator look distorted in one geometry and perfectly conservative in another?

A vector norm is not intrinsic to its coordinate entries: it depends on the inner product. In standard coordinates we use \(\|c\|_2^2=c^*c\); in a feature-space geometry we can instead use \(\|c\|_G^2=c^*Gc\). Thus, the statement that a transformation “preserves length” must always specify which length.

\[\|Mc\|_G^2=(Mc)^*G(Mc)=c^*(M^*GM)c.\]

Requiring this to equal \(\|c\|_G^2\) for every coefficient vector \(c\) yields the fundamental equation \(M^*GM=G\). This is the bridge from an intuitive geometric question to a precise matrix problem.

A concrete example

A rotation hidden by a change of coordinates

Start with an ordinary rotation \(R_\theta\), which preserves Euclidean length. Let \(S\) be invertible, and describe the same motion in distorted coordinates by \(M=SR_\theta S^{-1}\). In general \(M^*M\ne I\): Euclidean circles can appear stretched even though the underlying dynamics are rotational.

\[M=SR_\theta S^{-1},\qquad G=S^{-*}S^{-1}.\]

With this adapted metric, \(M^*GM=G\) exactly. The geometry compensates for the coordinate distortion without changing the operator. Section 05 lets you vary the rotation and shear and observe this construction.

Why the RKHS matters

From matrices to functions and kernels

In a finite-dimensional feature space, a function is represented by coefficients in a basis of features. A positive-definite Gram matrix determines their inner products, while its inverse appears in the corresponding reproducing kernel. Designing \(G\) therefore changes not only a matrix norm but the Hilbert-space geometry of the represented functions.

\[k_G(x,y)=\Psi(x)^*G^{-1}\Psi(y).\]

This makes the inverse problem relevant to representation learning: we seek feature-space geometries in which a prescribed linear action has a guaranteed structural property, rather than relying on an unconstrained learned representation.

Why the existence theorem is necessary

Not every operator admits an adapted positive-definite metric

Changing the inner product cannot erase genuine exponential growth, decay, or a nontrivial Jordan block. In finite dimensions, an invertible matrix admits a positive-definite solution of \(M^*GM=G\) if and only if it is diagonalizable over \(\mathbb C\) and all of its eigenvalues lie on the unit circle. This criterion separates geometries that can be designed from operators for which exact isometry is impossible.

\[\exists\,G\succ0:\ M^*GM=G\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad M\text{ is diagonalizable and }|\lambda_j(M)|=1\ \forall j.\]

The equivalence is for the finite-dimensional setting studied here; it is not a blanket statement about arbitrary infinite-dimensional operators.

What this enables

Structure-preserving representations

When the criterion holds, an adapted RKHS can encode exact norm preservation under the specified operator. This gives a principled geometric ingredient for stable operator representations, invariant feature dynamics, and controlled comparisons of learned coordinates.

These are motivations and possible uses of the construction, not claims that the paper establishes improved performance for every downstream learning task.

What makes the inverse view different

Classify the geometry, not just the transformation

Rather than checking whether a fixed kernel happens to make \(M\) isometric, we ask which kernels do. The admissible set of metrics can contain many choices; their classification reveals freedom to tailor a representation while preserving the same exact operator identity.

Central distinction. The operator, its eigenvalues, and its underlying action are fixed. The metric and associated reproducing kernel are designed. This is an exact finite-dimensional result, not a claim that arbitrary unstable operators can be made unitary.

02 / Mathematical foundations

From a prescribed operator to an exact RKHS isometry.

The central mathematical step is to translate a question about preserving the norm of functions into a matrix equation for the inner product. This section follows Proposition 2.1, Theorem 2.2, and Remark 2.3 of the accepted manuscript, with short derivations explaining why each condition matters.

Step 01 · The representation space

Fix the functions and the operator; leave the geometry free.

Choose linearly independent complex-valued functions \(\psi_1,\ldots,\psi_m\) on a nonempty set \(X\). Their span is a finite-dimensional function space. The prescribed linear operator \(T\) acts on coefficient vectors through a matrix \(M\).

\[V=\operatorname{span}\{\psi_1,\ldots,\psi_m\},\qquad f_c(x)=\Psi(x)^*c,\qquad Tf_c=f_{Mc}.\]

The coefficients \(c\) describe a function, but they do not determine how its length is measured. That additional choice is encoded by a Hermitian positive-definite matrix \(G\).

Step 02 · Choosing an inner product

A metric on coefficient space

\[G=G^*\succ0,\qquad \langle f_c,f_d\rangle_G=d^*Gc,\qquad\|f_c\|_G^2=c^*Gc.\]

Positive definiteness ensures that every nonzero function has positive squared norm. Changing \(G\) changes the geometry without changing the basis functions or the action of \(T\).

Step 03 · Reproducing the function values

Why this geometry defines an RKHS

\[k_G(x,y)=\Psi(x)^*G^{-1}\Psi(y).\]

Proposition 2.1. Every \(G\succ0\) yields a reproducing-kernel Hilbert space on the same finite-dimensional function space. The inverse matrix appears because kernel sections must represent point evaluation under the chosen inner product.

Derivation · The reproducing property

Where does the inverse Gram metric come from?

For a fixed \(y\in X\), the kernel section \(k_G(\cdot,y)\) has coefficient vector \(G^{-1}\Psi(y)\). Using the inner-product convention in the manuscript,

\[\langle f_c,k_G(\cdot,y)\rangle_G=(G^{-1}\Psi(y))^*Gc=\Psi(y)^*c=f_c(y).\]

This is the reproducing property. It makes the connection between a positive-definite matrix and a genuine function-space kernel exact—not merely an analogy with metric learning.

Step 04 · The invariant-metric equation

Norm preservation becomes a matrix identity.

Since \(Tf_c=f_{Mc}\), asking the operator to preserve the RKHS norm for every function is equivalent to asking that a quadratic form remain unchanged for every coefficient vector:

\[\|Tf_c\|_G^2=(Mc)^*G(Mc)=c^*M^*GMc.\]
\[\|Tf_c\|_G=\|f_c\|_G\ \text{for every }c\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\boxed{M^*GM=G}.\]

Theorem 2.2. This equation is necessary and sufficient for \(T\) to act isometrically on the constructed RKHS. It is a linear matrix constraint in the unknown entries of \(G\), together with the essential requirement \(G\succ0\).

Why existence is nontrivial

Not every operator can be made an isometry.

Theorem 2.2 further shows that a positive-definite solution exists if and only if \(M\) is similar to a unitary matrix. In finite dimensions, this is equivalent to two simultaneous requirements:

\[M\ \text{diagonalizable},\qquad\sigma(M)\subset\mathbb T,\quad \mathbb T=\{z\in\mathbb C:|z|=1\}.\]

Unit-modulus eigenvalues are necessary, but they are not sufficient on their own.

Remark 2.3 · The Jordan obstruction

Why eigenvalues alone can mislead

\[J=\begin{bmatrix}1&1\\0&1\end{bmatrix},\qquad J^n=\begin{bmatrix}1&n\\0&1\end{bmatrix}.\]

Every eigenvalue of \(J\) equals one, yet its powers grow. If \(J^*GJ=G\) held for a positive-definite \(G\), all powers would preserve the \(G\)-norm—contradicting this growth. A nontrivial Jordan block therefore obstructs exact adaptation.

Constructive proof idea

Similarity to a unitary matrix gives an explicit solution.

Suppose \(M=SDS^{-1}\), where \(S\) is invertible and \(D\) is diagonal with unit-modulus entries. Then \(D^*D=I\). Choose

\[G=S^{-*}S^{-1},\qquad S^{-*}=(S^{-1})^*.\]

This \(G\) is positive definite, and direct substitution gives

\[M^*GM=S^{-*}D^*DS^{-1}=S^{-*}S^{-1}=G.\]

Conversely, if \(M^*GM=G\), then \(U=G^{1/2}MG^{-1/2}\) satisfies \(U^*U=I\). Hence \(M\) is similar to a unitary matrix. These two directions explain the exact existence criterion.

What is preserved?

All inner products, not only norms

\[\langle Tf_c,Tf_d\rangle_G=d^*M^*GMc=d^*Gc.\]

Once the metric equation holds, the operator preserves pairwise inner products and the corresponding distances and angles in the adapted Hilbert geometry.

What is not being claimed?

The theorem has a precise scope

The construction applies to a fixed finite-dimensional invariant function space and a prescribed linear action on it. It does not imply that arbitrary dissipative, unstable, defective, or infinite-dimensional operators admit an exact positive-definite invariant RKHS metric.

Bridge to Section 03. The existence theorem answers whether an adapted RKHS can exist. It does not say there is only one. The next section classifies every admissible positive-definite metric and reproducing kernel, showing how repeated eigenvalues create additional geometric freedom.
03 / Classification of all solutions

Not one adapted geometry. An entire family.

Theorem 2.4 and Corollaries 2.5–2.6 describe every compatible metric and kernel. Repeated eigenvalues create additional degrees of freedom inside their eigenspaces.

Suppose \(M=SDS^{-1}\), with distinct eigenvalues \(\mu_1,\ldots,\mu_r\) of multiplicities \(m_1,\ldots,m_r\), all of modulus one. Then every invariant metric is of the form

\[G=S^{-*}\operatorname{diag}(H_1,\ldots,H_r)S^{-1},\qquad H_j\succ0.\]

The complete family of reproducing kernels is therefore

\[k_H(x,y)=\Psi(x)^*S\operatorname{diag}(H_1^{-1},\ldots,H_r^{-1})S^*\Psi(y).\]

Its real parameter-space dimension is

\[\dim_{\mathbb R}\mathcal G(M)=\sum_{j=1}^r m_j^2.\]
Simple spectrum

One scale per eigenvector

If every eigenvalue is distinct, each \(H_j\) is a positive scalar. The invariant family has \(m\) real degrees of freedom.

Repeated spectrum

More geometry within eigenspaces

An eigenspace of multiplicity \(m_j\) admits an \(m_j\times m_j\) Hermitian positive-definite block, contributing \(m_j^2\) real parameters.
